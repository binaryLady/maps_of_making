# The Overview Effect — UX North Stars for the Continental View

*A design compass for Maps of Making's stratospheric layer. Not a spec. A reminder of the feeling we're building toward, drawn from what astronauts report the first time they see Earth whole.*

---

The continental view is not a zoomed-out version of the map. It is a different experience with a different job. Up close, the map answers *"where can I make this thing near me?"* From altitude, it answers something only the joint graph can: *"how big is the thing I belong to?"* These north stars keep that second experience honest.

---

## 1. The borders aren't there

**What they report.** Almost every astronaut says it: the lines that organize life on the ground — that wars are fought over — simply do not exist from above. The land is continuous. The hard boundary turns out to be a fiction someone drew.

**Our principle.** The continental view never colors by network or country. Those *are* the drawn lines — the exact fragmentation MoM exists to dissolve. Spaces are kin by what they do and whether they're alive, not by which directory claimed them. The overview moment is the one where the tribes disappear and a single field remains.

**And the names dissolve too.** Not just borders — labels. At altitude the place-names, country names, region names fade out of the view. We are left with geography and the points: the shape of the land and the living lights on it. A name is another kind of drawn line — it sorts and claims. Stripping the labels is what lets the eye see *one field of making* instead of "spaces in France, spaces in Belgium." The map gets quieter as you rise, until only the breathing remains. *(GL consequence: the place/label symbol layers fade out below the continental zoom threshold — the same `maxzoom`/zoom-opacity machinery that swaps dots for aggregate lenses. Coastlines/landmass stay; named labels go.)*

---

## 2. From "find mine" to "see the whole"

**What they report.** On an international crew, the first day everyone pointed out their own country through the window. Days later they pointed to continents. By the end, they were aware only of one Earth.

**Our principle.** Design the arc, not just the arrival. People come looking for their own grey pin — the recognition jolt is the right first beat, and we honor it. But the experience must *lift* them off it: recognition, then belonging. The view's gravity pulls outward, from "there's my space" toward "look how many of us there are."

---

## 3. The thin blue line

**What they report.** The atmosphere is impossibly thin — a few miles of gauzy haze, all that stands between everything alive and the void. The dominant feeling isn't triumph. It's tenderness toward something fragile.

**Our principle.** Freshness is fragility, and we don't hide it. Aging, zombie, dead, broken — these aren't failures to suppress behind a clean facade. They are the thin blue line that makes the living, glowing spaces feel precious rather than assumed. A movement you can watch breathe, and sometimes falter, is one worth tending.

---

## 4. It lands in the body

**What they report.** Edgar Mitchell described an explosion of awareness he hadn't gone looking for. People weep. They go quiet. It isn't an argument they're persuaded by; it's a perspective that overtakes them.

**Our principle.** Restraint. The stratospheric view is still and quiet — dark field, slow glow, a pulse breathing — not a busy dashboard. Chrome is the enemy of awe. Let the moment land before offering controls. This is why the lens toggle stays low and unobtrusive: the surface is the sky, not the instrument panel.

---

## 5. Small enough to hold, large enough that no one is alone

**What they report.** Everything that ever meant anything — every birth, every work of art, all of history — sits on something you can cover with your thumb. Smallness and preciousness at once.

**Our principle.** One quiet counter does the work of a thousand pins: *"342 of you, across 11 countries, work this way."* Small enough to hold in a single frame; large enough that the number itself is the comfort. We resist the dashboard urge to surround it with metrics. One true sentence, well-earned, is the payload.

---

## 6. They come back protective

**What they report.** Astronauts return changed, and the change is a wanting — to protect the thing they saw.

**Our principle.** This is our answer to the maintenance problem every other directory failed to solve with nagging. If someone has *felt* themselves part of a living continental commons, keeping their own pin alive stops being a chore and becomes an act of care for something they belong to. The reciprocal embed is the mechanic. The overview moment is the reason it works. Belonging is the freshness incentive.

---

## 7. The dark around the light

**What they report.** Not everyone comes back euphoric. William Shatner described grief — the contrast between the warm living Earth and the cold death just beyond it.

**Our principle.** A map that only ever celebrates feels like marketing. One that lets you feel the dark around the light — the dead forts, the spaces gone quiet, the void at the edges — earns the awe it asks for. We do not airbrush the ecosystem into a brochure. Honesty about the dark is what makes the light trustworthy.

---

## The through-line

Every existing maker map is a tool for finding a place. The overview effect points at something else entirely: a tool for *feeling part of a movement*. The continental view's success isn't measured in clicks or filters applied. It's measured in a single felt shift — from *my workshop* to *our commons* — and in what that feeling makes people willing to tend.

> *Proximity and recognition reduce cognitive load. But altitude and belonging reduce loneliness. The map does both, at different heights.*
